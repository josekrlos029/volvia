It looks like a detail. It is not. The number of stamps decides how long a customer takes to reach the reward, and that length is what separates a card that gets finished from one that gets abandoned halfway.

## Start with the rhythm, not the margin

The temptation is to work backwards from what the reward costs you. That is the wrong first calculation. The first question is simpler: **how often does a customer who already likes you come back**.

An office coffee shop sees the same person four times a week. A dinner restaurant sees them every fortnight. A barbershop, every three weeks. With that figure, the stamp count stops being an opinion and becomes a division.

## The six-week rule

In practice, a card that takes more than six or eight weeks to finish stops feeling reachable. The customer no longer associates it with their next visit but with a vague future, and a vague reward does not change behaviour.

Divide six weeks by your visit frequency:

- Someone visiting four times a week comfortably reaches 10 stamps.
- Someone visiting weekly is better at 6.
- Someone visiting fortnightly needs 4, or an intermediate reward.

## When the number has to be high

Sometimes the margin does not allow a reward every six visits. The answer is not to stretch the card and hope: it is to **split it**. A small reward halfway changes the feeling of progress entirely.

A ten-stamp card with something at the fifth reads as two short cards, not one long one. The total cost can be identical and the completion rate is not.

## What to watch after a month

Once the card has run for a few weeks, two numbers tell you whether you got it right:

**How many get past the first stamp.** If lots of people stop at one, the length is not the problem: you did not explain what they win.

**How many reach the end.** If most stall midway, the card is long for your real rhythm. Shortening it is easier than convincing anyone to visit more often.

## What not to do

Do not change the length of a card people are already filling. Moving the goal mid-game is the fastest way to lose your best visitors. If you need a different setup, create a new card and let the old one finish its cycle.
